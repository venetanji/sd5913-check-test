# Why are we here?

The course assumes I'm either a designer or an artist. I'm actually somewhere in between: I'm a developer (more specifically, I have a B.Sc. in Video Game Programming). As a lead developer at a small indie company, I have worn multiple hats, handling marketing, project management, programming, art, and technical leadership.

Although I do touch art, design, sound, project management, and other areas of development, my main role has been programming. I have used AI to speed up processes that are unfamiliar to me or outside my area of expertise, such as changing the pose of a 3D model for marketing purposes or updating the text in an image without opening the source PSD file. For programming specifically, I tend not to rely on agents, but instead use AI as a search engine on steroids. It was extremely frustrating to look for a bug online and find an old forum post with no solution at all. With AI becoming more common, this practice has been increasingly under the microscope. While some may have been skeptical about AI a year ago, they will soon discover (if they haven't already) that generative AI is not going anywhere; it is here to stay.

It is a bizarre thing to think about: imagine entering a painting class only to discover that you can rely entirely on AI to paint. No brush is needed, and you don't get your hands dirty.

At the same time, it is almost impossible not to rely on AI for coding, so **finding a balance between reckless vibe coding and using AI as a tool is what I hope to learn from this course. As I understand it, the course is not about learning how to make a hammer, but about learning how to use it properly in real-world scenarios—especially when my classmates are artists and designers rather than engineers.** Based on my experience, you need to:

1. Know what you're asking for. You can't just ask a clanker to paint a masterpiece. What shape, what style, how many? This is where your creativity comes into play.
2. Be able to read the output. In the real world, you won't be working on small class projects or game jams, and you will soon discover that vibe coding a product in a few weeks will make you little to no profit at all. You will end up with dozens of repositories with no real market. What you will end up doing instead is writing (or prompting) maintainable code that you and your teammates can read months from now. Why would a company hire you? They can just replace you with an AI agent. You have to learn to use the environment, read code, fix bugs, and identify weak points.
3. Understand that times are changing. After all, programming languages are a way of communicating with the machine. In this case, we are at the highest level possible, using natural English language. Failing to understand this will leave your competition light years ahead of you.
4. Do not oversimplify things. During the Industrial Revolution, many jobs were eliminated, but different jobs were created. Your efforts were redirected elsewhere. Unfortunately, this is no longer the case; the new wave of AI has brought a new wave of layoffs. Knowing where to put your efforts is very important.
5. Take pride in your creation. As controversial as it sounds, you were the one who prompted it, so the creation is yours. On the same note, know when to get off your high horse: if two people can give the same prompt, what makes your creation better than the rest?
